Anomalous Voltage Responses Preceding Thermal Runaway in Cylindrical Lithium‑Ion Batteries

An experimental investigation of unusual terminal-voltage drops and recoveries before rapid thermal runaway, and their potential value as early warning signals.

Tianli Shi · Department of Chemical Engineering · University College London · Supervisor: Alex Rettie
Explore the findingsView thesis structure
Abstract

Voltage anomalies appeared before rapid thermal runaway

A 100% state-of-charge B18650CX‑20M cylindrical lithium-ion cell was studied using Accelerating Rate Calorimetry (ARC), with complementary X-ray CT, XRD, DSC and TGA characterisation. Two discrete voltage drops appeared before the final rapid temperature rise. The first was from approximately 3.98 V to 2.45–2.50 V. The second was from approximately 2.25 V to 0.20 V, followed by a recovery to roughly 3.4–3.5 V. The drop-and-recovery sequence differs from a simple continuous discharge or permanent hard internal short and motivates investigation of transient electrical-path changes and cell protection-device behaviour.

Materials characterisation

Structural and thermal context for the electrical response

X-ray CT

CT images showed the positive terminal cap, crimped casing edge, wound jelly-roll core and steel housing. The cell architecture remained identifiable and provided a structural baseline for interpreting later failure behaviour.

XRD

Diffraction results supported a mixed cathode composition containing olivine-type LMFP together with a layered transition-metal oxide component.

DSC

Differential scanning calorimetry showed a temperature-dependent thermal response in the approximate 95–120 °C region, providing context for reactions occurring before severe thermal runaway.

TGA

Measured mass loss was approximately 2.7–2.9%. In the thesis this was not considered sufficient, by itself, to explain the first large voltage drop as continuous bulk cathode decomposition.

Core finding

The voltage trajectory is non-monotonic

Stage 1 · slow self-heating

The cell initially shows gradual voltage decline while self-heating remains relatively low.

Drop 1 · ≈19.6 h · ≈110 °C

Terminal voltage falls from about 3.98 V to 2.45–2.50 V, a drop of roughly 1.5 V or 37–38% of the pre-event voltage. There is no clear sharp synchronous temperature rise.

Drop 2 · ≈23.6 h · ≈125–130 °C

Voltage falls from about 2.25 V to about 0.20 V, corresponding to roughly 91% of the pre-event terminal voltage.

Recovery · ≈23.7–26.2 h

The terminal voltage subsequently recovers to approximately 3.4–3.5 V while self-heating accelerates. This behaviour is inconsistent with a simple permanent hard short.

Final stage · rapid thermal runaway

Self-heating rises by orders of magnitude and the test proceeds into thermal runaway. Data recorded after ADC overload are not treated as quantitatively reliable.

Mechanistic interpretation

What might cause the anomalous voltage?

Low-temperature side reactions

Electrochemical side reactions and evolving internal conditions may alter impedance and available electrochemical pathways before the cell enters full runaway.

Pressure and contact changes

Volatilisation and gas generation can increase internal pressure and may contribute to transient changes in electrical contact inside the cylindrical cell.

Possible CID activation

The recovery pattern is compatible with a transient interruption of the electrical path, including possible current-interrupt-device activation.

Why a permanent hard short is insufficient

A permanent severe internal short would not naturally explain a large voltage collapse followed by substantial recovery to 3.4–3.5 V. The observed sequence therefore points toward a transient or changing electrical pathway.

Evidence boundary

The measurements cannot uniquely distinguish safety-device activation from a localised or transient internal short circuit. CT, XRD, DSC and TGA were not recorded in situ during the exact voltage-drop events, so the thesis does not claim a single directly proven mechanism.

Conclusions

Electrical anomalies may offer earlier warning than temperature alone

Safety implicationVoltage, surface temperature and self-heating rate may be combined for multi-signal early warning.
Future work: replicated tests across multiple cells and abuse conditions, together with in-situ structural/electrical diagnostics, are needed to determine the precise mechanism and establish how robust the voltage-drop/recovery signature is as a practical warning indicator.
